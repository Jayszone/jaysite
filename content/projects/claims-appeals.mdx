## Problem

Most claims are adjudicated automatically. A claim that fails an automated check is routed to an analyst as an exception, and the analyst has to find the procedure that applies to it.

Those procedures (SOPs) were spread across several knowledge bases, in PDF, HTML, Markdown and other formats. Some PDFs ran from 2 to 80 pages, and a complex claim could require 4 to 10 of them. Search was keyword-based and returned whole documents, not answers. Analysts read each document to find the relevant passage, compared versions when documents disagreed, and rechecked their conclusion before submitting it.

Appeals had the same problem in a different workflow. Representatives answering policy and procedure questions by phone or chat had to look up the guidance while the customer waited.

## What I built

I was the product manager for an AI assistant that answers questions from these documents. It uses retrieval-augmented generation (RAG). Documents are converted to a consistent format, split into chunks and stored in a searchable vector index. When someone asks a question, the system retrieves the most relevant chunks and generates an answer from them.

Version 1 returned answers only. Experienced analysts did not use it, because they could not trace an answer back to a source and still verified everything by hand. Version 2 added citations: each answer links to the source document and the exact section it came from. The analyst can read the evidence and decide whether it applies.

Three other problems took most of the engineering time:

- **Ingestion.** The documents came in several formats. We converted some PDFs to Markdown and tested fixed-size chunks against chunks that follow section boundaries, which keep a heading with the text under it.
- **Versions.** Several versions of the same SOP existed, and the assistant could return outdated guidance. We changed retrieval to prioritize newer documents using timestamps. This is imperfect, because a timestamp shows when a file was updated, not which policy applied on the claim's date of service. Explicit effective dates and supersession rules would handle it properly.
- **Decision authority.** The assistant retrieves and explains guidance. Analysts make the claim decision, and complex cases still go to senior review.

We then reused the same retrieval and answer capability for appeals, so representatives could answer questions during a call or chat with the supporting source shown.

## Results

- Time spent on the primary investigation step, finding and checking the applicable guidance, fell by about 80%. This is the lookup step, not the whole case. Secondary verification and senior review are unchanged, so total handling time falls by less.
- Adoption rose by about 20% after citations were added.
- Appeals representatives could get cited answers while the customer was still on the line.

## Business impact

The business case was about $4M in annual savings across claims and appeals, based on the time returned to analysts and representatives.

## What I would change

I would include citations and policy effective dates in the first version. Analysts needed the citations to trust the output, and retrieval needed the dates to return the right version of each procedure. I would also measure the full workflow from the start, with a baseline and a comparison group, so the savings can be attributed to the product with more confidence.
