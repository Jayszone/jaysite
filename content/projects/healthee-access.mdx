## Problem

Employers pay for health insurance so employees can get care, but some routine services cost less when paid for directly than when billed through insurance. The gap varies by service and market, and direct pay is not always cheaper.

Using a direct-pay provider also means doing steps that insurance normally handles. The employee needs a physician order when one is required, a provider that accepts direct payment, an appointment, and a way to pay. The open question was whether employees would use a program that took care of those steps for them.

## What I built

Healthee Access is an employer-sponsored program that lets employees and their dependents book lower-cost diagnostic, preventive and outpatient services outside insurance. There was no existing product or playbook, and I owned the pilot from scope through day-to-day operation.

- **Scope.** I defined the initial operating model and grew it to 27 procedures: imaging and radiology, lab testing, OB-GYN, cardiac imaging, orthopedics, office visits and other outpatient care.
- **Participant journey.** Eligibility, physician order, provider, booking, payment by voucher or direct payment with no claim, then support and feedback. I mapped each step and ran it during the pilot.
- **Friction points.** Participants got stuck most often on physician orders, provider acceptance and booking. I redesigned those workflows and wrote repeatable processes for failed bookings, provider issues, order delays and payment questions.
- **Physician orders.** I compared telehealth consultations, internal clinical support, provider referrals and direct booking, and coordinated orders for about half of the pilot's services.
- **Cost.** I managed a $100,000 budget and delivered the pilot for about $70,000. Choices weighed price against employee experience and how hard the service was to operate. The cheapest provider was not always the right one.

The pilot depended on Product, Benefits Support, HR, Finance, Sales, Customer Success and outside providers. None of them reported to me, so progress depended on making specific requests early and following up.

## Pilot results

- 100 employees and dependents were invited. 90 registered.
- About 85% used the program, and 92% of users came back to use it again.
- 96% gave feedback. That response rate came from personalized outreach to each team and direct support through enrollment and booking, not a standard survey link.
- The pilot finished about $30,000, or 30%, under budget.

I used feedback and usage patterns during the pilot to improve messaging, procedure discovery, booking support and the physician-order process.

## Business case

I analyzed more than 5,000 claims data points from a large employer client to find the high-cost categories that direct access could address, and modeled adoption at 10%, 20% and 30%.

The analysis identified up to $7.79 million in potential savings. This is a modeled opportunity based on historical claims, not realized savings. Realizing less than 0.5% of it would cover the program's cost. I used the analysis, the pilot data and procedure-level costs to write pricing and rollout recommendations and presented them to company stakeholders. The client deal was secured.

## What I took from it

Most of the pilot's problems were handoffs: physician orders, provider acceptance and payment. The idea itself held up. I learned to ask other teams for specific things early, because most of the people the pilot depended on did not report to me.

It also changed how I judge analytical work. At UnitedHealth I measured a model by how well it performed. Here the claims analysis mattered because of the decision it supported.
